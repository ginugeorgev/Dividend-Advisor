📐 Walter Dividend Model
James Walter (1963) — Dividend policy is relevant when r ≠ Ke

📖 Walter's Theory

Walter's model states that the theoretical value of a share depends on the relationship between:

  • Return on Investment (r) — the firm's earnings rate on retained profits
  • Cost of Equity (Ke) — the shareholders' required rate of return
  • Earnings per Share (E) — the profit attributable per share
  • Dividend per Share (D) — the amount paid to each shareholder
P = [D + (r / Ke)(E − D)] / Ke

Where:

  • P = Theoretical market value per share
  • D = Dividend per share
  • E = Earnings per share
  • r = Internal rate of return (ROI)
  • Ke = Cost of equity (required return)

💡 Model Interpretation

📋 Model Assumptions

View Walter Model Assumptions▼
  • The firm finances all investment through retained earnings (no external financing)
  • The firm's internal rate of return (r) and cost of equity (Ke) are constant
  • All earnings are either distributed as dividends or reinvested internally
  • The firm has an infinite life
  • Capital markets are perfect (no taxes, no flotation costs)
  • EPS and DPS are constant (no change over time)
⚠️ These are idealized theoretical assumptions. Real-world dividend decisions involve taxes, market imperfections, and strategic considerations.

📈 Gordon Growth Model
Myron Gordon (1962) — Share value based on expected dividends and growth

📖 Gordon's Theory

Gordon's model values a share based on the present value of all future dividends, assuming a constant growth rate. The share price is determined by:

P = D₁ / (Ke − g)

Where D₁ = D₀ × (1 + g)

And g = b × r

Variables:

  • P = Theoretical share value
  • D₀ = Current dividend per share
  • D₁ = Expected dividend next period
  • Ke = Cost of equity / required return
  • g = Constant growth rate = b × r
  • b = Retention ratio (1 − payout ratio)
  • r = Return on investment
⚠️ Critical Condition: The Gordon model requires g < Ke. If the growth rate equals or exceeds the cost of equity, the model cannot produce a meaningful finite value.

⚖️ Modigliani-Miller Dividend Irrelevance Theory
Franco Modigliani & Merton Miller (1961) — Dividend policy is irrelevant under idealized assumptions

📖 The MM Proposition

Modigliani and Miller argued that under certain idealized assumptions, dividend policy does not affect the total value of the firm. The value of the firm depends primarily on its earning power and investment policy, not on how profits are split between dividends and retained earnings.

Under MM, if a firm pays higher dividends, it must raise an equivalent amount through external financing. The gain from dividends is exactly offset by the loss in value from issuing new shares. Hence, shareholders are indifferent between dividends and capital gains.

📐 MM Formula

P₀ = (D₁ + P₁) / (1 + Ke)
  • P₀ = Current share value (beginning of period)
  • D₁ = Dividend at end of period
  • P₁ = Expected share value at end of period
  • Ke = Cost of equity (required rate of return)
💡 Under MM, the combination of D₁ + P₁ remains constant regardless of how much dividend is paid. An increase in D₁ is exactly offset by a decrease in P₁ (because external financing dilutes the value).

🏛️ MM Assumptions

MM's dividend irrelevance theory depends on these idealized assumptions. In practice, most of these do not fully hold, which is why dividend policy still matters in real-world finance.

💰 External Financing Requirement

Under MM, if a company pays more dividends than it retains, it must raise equivalent external financing. This exactly offsets the benefit of dividends.

External Financing = Investment Requirement − Retained Earnings

AI Dividend Policy Advisor — Version 1.0

An interactive educational web application for B.Com, M.Com, and MBA Finance students. Covers Walter Model, Gordon Growth Model, and Modigliani-Miller Dividend Irrelevance Theory.

Uses Chart.js for visualizations, jsPDF for PDF export, SheetJS for Excel export, and localStorage for history. Runs entirely in the browser — no data is sent to any server.
